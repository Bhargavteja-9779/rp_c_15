# 6. How far standard error bars fail on later seasons

## 6.1 Point accuracy and the structure of the error

Table 2 gives the error of the three point predictors. Cross-validation with sample-grouped folds, the usual way of reporting a calibration, gives the smallest error; lot-grouped folds give a larger one and season-grouped folds the largest. The error obtained on the next season exceeds the sample-fold estimate for every target season, and from 2018 on it exceeds the season-fold estimate as well. The standard deviation of the lot mean residual in the target seasons is {{f"{r.t['table2_point_models'].query('model==\"pls\"').sd_lot_bias.min():.2f}"}}–{{f"{r.t['table2_point_models'].query('model==\"pls\"').sd_lot_bias.max():.2f}"}} % DMC for PLS, of the same order as the within-lot error. Fig. 3 shows both effects: the residual distribution on the next season is wider than the sample-fold cross-validation residuals, and lots scatter around zero with season-specific offsets. The variance components estimated by Eq. (2) from season-held-out residuals agree: the between-lot standard deviation $\hat\tau$ is {{r.tau_sigma()['tau']}} % DMC and the within-lot, between-sample standard deviation $\hat\sigma$ is {{r.tau_sigma()['sigma']}} % DMC across the five source sets.

## 6.2 Intervals without reference samples (RQ1)

Table 3 lists the intervals that need no reference sample. Split conformal prediction with sample-grouped folds covers {{r.c('scp_sample',0)}} of the reference values on later-season lots (95 % CI {{r.ci('scp_sample',0)}}) at a nominal 0.90, and {{r.u('scp_sample',0)}} % of the lots have coverage below 0.80. The Gaussian band, the quantile regression forest and conformalised quantile regression with sample folds behave similarly (coverage {{r.c('gauss',0)}}, {{r.c('qrf',0)}} and {{r.c('cqr_sample',0)}}). The deep ensemble and Monte-Carlo dropout give the narrowest intervals and the lowest coverage ({{r.c('ensemble',0)}} and {{r.c('mc_dropout',0)}}): their predictive variance reflects the training seasons, not the lot effect of a new one. Their interval score is nevertheless the best in the table, because the CNN underlying them has a lower point error than PLS (Table 2). The shortfall is not produced by the lot-balanced weighting: with every scan weighted equally, sample-fold split conformal prediction covers {{r.unweighted('scp_sample')}}. H1 is confirmed.

{{T:main0}}

The hold-out unit orders coverage as hypothesised (H2): sample folds {{r.c('scp_sample',0)}}, lot folds {{r.c('scp_lot',0)}}, season folds {{r.c('scp_season',0)}}, and the ordering holds in each of the five target seasons (Table 5). Holding out lots repairs only a small part of the gap, which shows that the lot effect within the training seasons is smaller than the lot effect of a new season. Season-held-out residuals bring the pooled coverage to the nominal level, for split conformal prediction and for conformalised quantile regression alike ({{r.c('cqr_season',0)}}). Two qualifications are needed. First, the price is width: intervals are {{r.pct(r.m('scp_season',0,'width'), r.m('scp_sample',0,'width'))}} % wider than with sample folds, and the interval score is not better ({{r.i('scp_season',0)}} against {{r.i('scp_sample',0)}}). Second, the pooled figure hides season-to-season variation: coverage by season ranges over {{r.season_range('scp_season',0)}} (Table 5), with over-coverage in 2017, when only two source seasons were available and the season-held-out models were weak, and coverage below nominal in each of the four later seasons. With a handful of past seasons, the next season is simply not exchangeable with them to within a few coverage points. LotCP and LotCP-S at $m=0$ behave like season-fold split conformal prediction, as expected.

Weighted conformal prediction with likelihood-ratio weights from the unlabelled target spectra reaches {{r.c('wcp',0)}} coverage, but with intervals {{r.pct(r.m('wcp',0,'width'), r.m('scp_sample',0,'width'))}} % wider than split conformal prediction and unbounded intervals for {{f"{100*r.m('wcp',0,'frac_infinite'):.0f}"}} % of scans: when a lot is spectrally distinct, the estimated weights concentrate on few calibration points. It has the worst interval score of all methods. Covariate-shift weighting is not the right tool here, because the shift that matters is a lot bias in the residual, not a change in the distribution of spectra alone.

# 7. Few-shot calibration with reference fruit

## 7.1 Intervals with reference samples (RQ2)

Table 4 and Fig. 4 show what $m$ reference samples per lot buy. Three samples are enough to change the picture: every method that corrects the lot bias then covers at least 0.90 with intervals of about the same width as the invalid sample-fold split conformal intervals. The corrected point prediction improves accordingly: the lot-level RMSE of PLS falls from {{f"{r.point(0,'none'):.2f}"}} % DMC without correction to {{f"{r.point(3,'shrinkage'):.2f}"}} with three and {{f"{r.point(10,'shrinkage'):.2f}"}} with ten reference samples.

{{T:mainm}}

![Fig. 4. Lot-macro coverage, mean width and interval score as a function of the number of reference samples per lot (PLS, forward chaining, {{V:n_target_lots}} target lots, nominal 0.90). Dashed lines: split conformal prediction without reference samples.](figures/fig4_main.png){width=6.5in}

*Methods that do not correct the bias.* Pooling the reference residuals into the source calibration set has no visible effect (coverage {{r.c('pooled',10)}} at $m=10$): a few target residuals among tens of thousands of source residuals do not move the quantile. Adaptive conformal inference over the reference samples raises coverage to {{r.c('aci',10)}} at $m=10$ but does so by widening the interval, and returns unbounded intervals in {{f"{100*r.m('aci',10,'frac_infinite'):.0f}"}} % of lot-draws.

*Target-only calibration.* Split conformal prediction on the reference samples alone is valid within each lot, which shows in its tight distribution of lot-wise coverage (Fig. 5). It cannot return a bounded 90 % interval for $m<9$, and at $m=10$ it is conservative (coverage {{r.c('target_only_centred',10)}}) and wide: the centred version is {{r.pct(r.m('target_only_centred',10,'width'), r.m('lotcp_s',10,'width'))}} % wider than LotCP-S. By $m=20$ the centred version reaches the interval score of LotCP-S ({{r.i('target_only_centred',20)}} against {{r.i('lotcp_s',20)}}) and at $m=30$ it is marginally better ({{r.i('target_only_centred',30)}} against {{r.i('lotcp_s',30)}}).

*Bias correction with the source quantile.* Subtracting the mean reference residual and keeping the sample-fold half-width is a strong baseline with PLS. Its coverage is above nominal ({{r.c('biascorr_naive',5)}}–{{r.c('biascorr_naive',20)}}) because two errors partly cancel: the sample-fold residuals contain a remnant of the lot effect, which widens the interval, and the uncertainty of the estimated bias is ignored, which should narrow it. The half-width does not depend on $m$, so the method cannot turn additional reference samples into narrower intervals, and Section 12 shows that the cancellation does not carry over to other predictors.

*Slope-and-bias correction and spiking.* Two further maintenance practices were tested. Regressing the reference values of the $m$ samples on their predictions and applying the fitted slope and intercept is worse than the mean correction at $m=5$ (interval score {{r.i('slopebias_naive',5)}} against {{r.i('biascorr_naive',5)}}), because five points do not determine a slope, and is comparable from $m=10$ ({{r.i('slopebias_naive',10)}} against {{r.i('biascorr_naive',10)}}). Refitting the PLS model with ten reference samples added to the training set ("spiking") barely changes the predictions when the samples are added once (lot-level RMSE {{f"{r.spk('spiking_x1'):.2f}"}} % DMC against {{f"{r.spk('none'):.2f}"}} without any correction) and remains clearly worse than the mean bias correction when they are replicated twenty times ({{f"{r.spk('spiking_x20'):.2f}"}} against {{f"{r.spk('bias_correction'):.2f}"}}; supplementary table S11).

*LotCP and LotCP-S.* LotCP covers {{f"{min(r.m('lotcp',m) for m in (3,5,10,20,30)):.3f}"}}–{{f"{max(r.m('lotcp',m) for m in (3,5,10,20,30)):.3f}"}}, somewhat above nominal, with a half-width that shrinks as $m$ grows. LotCP-S covers {{f"{min(r.m('lotcp_s',m) for m in (3,5,10,20,30)):.3f}"}}–{{f"{max(r.m('lotcp_s',m) for m in (3,5,10,20,30)):.3f}"}} for $m\ge3$ and has the lowest interval score of all methods for $m$ from 3 to 20 (Table 4). At $m=10$ its intervals are {{r.pct(r.m('lotcp_s',10,'width'), r.m('biascorr_naive',10,'width'))}} % narrower than those of bias correction with the source quantile and {{r.pct(r.m('lotcp_s',10,'width'), r.m('lotcp',10,'width'))}} % narrower than those of LotCP. By season, its coverage at $m=10$ ranges over {{r.season_range('lotcp_s',10)}} (Table 5). Leaving out one target season at a time moves the pooled figure between {{f"{r.jk('lotcp_s',10,'jackknife_min'):.3f}"}} and {{f"{r.jk('lotcp_s',10,'jackknife_max'):.3f}"}}, and the unweighted mean over seasons is {{f"{r.jk('lotcp_s',10,'season_mean_unweighted'):.3f}"}} (supplementary table S4b); for sample-fold split conformal prediction the corresponding figures are {{f"{r.jk('scp_sample',0,'jackknife_min'):.3f}"}}–{{f"{r.jk('scp_sample',0,'jackknife_max'):.3f}"}} and {{f"{r.jk('scp_sample',0,'season_mean_unweighted'):.3f}"}}. The 2018 value is below nominal even though 2018 served as development data for the scale estimator; we return to this in Supplementary Note S3.

![Fig. 5. Coverage within each of the {{V:n_target_lots}} target lots (PLS, nominal 0.90, dotted line). Marginal coverage near the nominal level can coexist with many badly covered lots.](figures/fig5_lot_coverage.png){width=6.5in}

*Paired tests.* Supplementary table S20 compares the per-lot interval score of LotCP-S with its competitors. Against bias correction with the source quantile the difference is not significant at $m=3$ (Holm-adjusted p {{r.p('biascorr_naive',3)}}) and is significant from $m=5$ on (p {{r.p('biascorr_naive',5)}} at $m=5$, p {{r.p('biascorr_naive',10)}} at $m=10$), with the mean difference favouring LotCP-S in all five seasons from $m=5$. Against the centred target-only method LotCP-S is better at $m=10$ (p {{r.p('target_only_centred',10)}}), marginally better at $m=20$ (p {{r.p('target_only_centred',20)}}, mean difference {{f"{r.test('target_only_centred',20).mean_diff:.2f}"}}) and not different at $m=30$ (p {{r.p('target_only_centred',30)}}). On absolute coverage error the centred target-only method is better than LotCP-S at $m=20$ and $m=30$ (mean difference {{f"{r.test('target_only_centred',20,'abs_cov_err').mean_diff:.3f}"}} and {{f"{r.test('target_only_centred',30,'abs_cov_err').mean_diff:.3f}"}}; all tests are in the supplementary table S6), which reflects its lot-conditional validity. The conclusion for RQ2 is therefore bounded: LotCP-S is the best of the compared methods when few reference samples are available, roughly $3\le m\le 10$, and offers no advantage over target-only calibration once 20 to 30 samples per lot can be afforded.


## 7.2 Lot-level assurance

The marginal method leaves a minority of lots badly covered (Section 14 and Supplementary Note S3), and near-nominal average coverage means that in a large share of lots the realised coverage is below the nominal level: with LotCP-S and ten reference samples only {{r.meet('lotcp_s',10)}} % of lot-draws reach 0.90. Table 14 and Fig. 12 show what LotCP-A (Section 4.8) achieves when it is asked for 0.90 coverage in at least 90 % of lots.

With $\delta=0.10$, the share of lot-draws that reach 0.90 coverage is {{r.meet('lotcp_a',3)}} %, {{r.meet('lotcp_a',5)}} %, {{r.meet('lotcp_a',10)}} %, {{r.meet('lotcp_a',20)}} % and {{r.meet('lotcp_a',30)}} % for $m=3$, 5, 10, 20 and 30, against a target of 90 %; with $\delta=0.20$ it is {{r.meet('lotcp_a20',5)}} % and {{r.meet('lotcp_a20',10)}} % at $m=5$ and 10, against a target of 80 %. The share of lot-draws with coverage below 0.80 falls from {{r.u('lotcp_s',10)}} % under LotCP-S to {{r.u('lotcp_a',10)}} % at $m=10$. By target season the share reaching 0.90 at $m=10$ ranges over {{r.season_meets('lotcp_a',10)}} % ({{r.season_meets('lotcp_s',10)}} % for LotCP-S), so the assurance, like the marginal coverage, is met on average over seasons and missed by a few points in the less favourable ones. Across the other predictors and the leave-one-season-out protocol the share at $m=10$ is {{f"{100*min(r.gen(a,b,'lotcp_a',10,'meets') for a,b in [('gbm','forward'),('cnn','forward'),('pls','loso'),('cnn','loso')]):.0f}"}}–{{f"{100*max(r.gen(a,b,'lotcp_a',10,'meets') for a,b in [('gbm','forward'),('cnn','forward'),('pls','loso'),('cnn','loso')]):.0f}"}} %. Without reference samples the assurance is not attained ({{r.meet('lotcp_a',0)}} %) and the intervals are very wide ({{r.w('lotcp_a',0)}} % DMC).

The price is width. At $m=10$ the LotCP-A interval is {{r.pct(r.m('lotcp_a',10,'width'), r.m('lotcp_s',10,'width'))}} % wider than that of LotCP-S ({{r.w('lotcp_a',10)}} against {{r.w('lotcp_s',10)}} % DMC) and at $m=20$ it is {{r.pct(r.m('lotcp_a',20,'width'), r.m('lotcp_s',20,'width'))}} % wider. None of the alternatives offers the property. Bias correction with the source quantile reaches 0.90 in {{r.meet('biascorr_naive',10)}} % of lot-draws at $m=10$. Target-only calibration is valid within each lot only on average over reference draws, so the realised coverage of a given draw scatters around the nominal level: the centred version reaches 0.90 in {{r.meet('target_only_centred',10)}} % of lot-draws at $m=10$, where it is conservative and {{r.pct(r.m('target_only_centred',10,'width'), r.m('lotcp_a',10,'width'))}} % wider than LotCP-A, and in only {{r.meet('target_only_centred',20)}} % and {{r.meet('target_only_centred',30)}} % at $m=20$ and 30. At twenty reference samples LotCP-A is the only method examined that keeps nine lots in ten at the stated coverage, which is the regime in which the marginal comparison of Section 7.1 found no advantage for LotCP-S over target-only calibration.

On the soil data the share of lots reaching 0.90 at $m=10$ is {{f"{100*r.soil('kssl_oc','lotcp_a',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} % for held-out KSSL projects, where lots are exchangeable, and {{f"{100*r.soil('lucas_oc','lotcp_a',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} % and {{f"{100*r.soil('lucas_ph','lotcp_a',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} % for LUCAS organic carbon and pH, where the between-campaign change of residual scale again costs a few points; for LotCP-S the corresponding shares are {{f"{100*r.soil('kssl_oc','lotcp_s',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} %, {{f"{100*r.soil('lucas_oc','lotcp_s',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} % and {{f"{100*r.soil('lucas_ph','lotcp_s',10,'meets'):.0f}"}} % (supplementary table S13).

LotCP-S and LotCP-A answer different questions with the same calibration data. The first gives the narrowest interval that is right on average over lots; the second gives an interval that is right for the lot in hand in nine cases out of ten. For a decision that is taken lot by lot, the second statement is the one that corresponds to the risk the grower carries.
