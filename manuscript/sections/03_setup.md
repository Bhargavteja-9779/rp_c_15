# 5. Experimental setup

## 5.1 Data

We use the public "Mango DMC and NIR spectra" data set, version 6 [@mangodata] (CC BY 4.0), which extends the data of Anderson et al. [@anderson2020; @anderson2021] and Walsh et al. [@walsh2024] with further seasons and instrument units. It contains {{V:n_spectra}} spectra of intact mango fruit acquired with handheld spectrometers in interactance geometry [@anderson2021], each paired with the oven-dry DMC of the tissue under the scanned position. The file identifies the harvest season ({{V:n_seasons}} seasons, 2015–2021), the growing region (Northern Territory or Queensland, Australia), the cultivar ({{V:n_cultivars}} cultivars), the physiological stage, the fruit temperature class, the instrument unit ({{V:n_instruments}} units), the reference sample ({{V:n_samples}} samples) and the population ({{V:n_lots}} populations). A population is a set of fruit from one orchard block and harvest date; we call it a lot. Every sample was scanned repeatedly (median {{V:median_scans}} scans per sample; different instrument units, and for a subset three fruit temperatures). Table 1 summarises the data by season and Fig. 2 shows the distribution of reference values, the number of lots and the mean spectra.

We use the 684–990 nm window at 3 nm steps (103 variables), the range that gave the best PLS results in the original study [@anderson2020]. All spectra are retained. The provider's outlier flags are not applied in the main analysis because we cannot establish that they were assigned without reference to prediction residuals; their effect is examined in Supplementary Note S1.

## 5.2 Protocols and leakage control

*Forward chaining (primary).* For each target season $T\in\{2017,\dots,2021\}$ the source data are all seasons before $T$. Nothing from season $T$ or later enters training, preprocessing, hyper-parameter selection or calibration, except the $m$ reference samples of the lot under test. This gives {{V:n_target_lots}} target lots with at least 20 samples.

*Leave-one-season-out (secondary).* Each of the seven seasons is the target in turn and all other seasons are the source. This protocol uses later seasons to predict earlier ones and is reported as a robustness check only.

*Further shifts.* (i) Only the two seasons preceding $T$ as source; (ii) one growing region as target and the other as source; (iii) one cultivar as target and all others as source (five cultivars with enough lots).

Repeated scans of a sample share one reference value, so a random split of spectra would place near-duplicates on both sides. All folds are grouped: a sample never straddles a fold in the sample-grouped scheme, a lot never does in the lot-grouped scheme, and a season never does in the season-grouped scheme. The data provider's warning against random splitting is thereby respected. Reference samples are drawn at the sample level, and all scans of a drawn sample are removed from the evaluation set of that draw. Spectral pre-treatments are row-wise and learn nothing from other spectra; scaling constants of the neural network are computed on the training fold only.

## 5.3 Point predictors

*PLS.* Standard normal variate [@barnes1989] followed by a Savitzky–Golay second derivative (17-point window, second-order polynomial) [@savitzky1964] and partial least squares regression [@wold2001]. The number of latent variables is chosen from $\{8,10,\dots,20\}$ by leave-one-season-out cross-validation inside the source data.

*LightGBM.* Gradient-boosted trees [@ke2017] on the same pre-treated spectra (400 trees, learning rate 0.05, 31 leaves, minimum 40 samples per leaf, row subsampling 0.8, column subsampling 0.5).

*1D-CNN.* Three convolution layers (16, 32 and 64 filters; kernel sizes 7, 5 and 3; strides 1, 2 and 2; ELU activations), a 64-unit dense layer with dropout 0.1 and a linear output ({{V:cnn_params}} parameters), in the spirit of compact networks used for NIR regression [@cui2018; @walsh2024]. Inputs are SNV spectra standardised per wavelength. Training uses AdamW (weight decay $10^{-4}$), a one-cycle learning-rate schedule peaking at $10^{-3}$, batch size 256 and 20 epochs. No early stopping is used, so no validation split is needed.

LightGBM and the CNN are run with three seeds fixed before the experiments (0, 1, 2). All results are reported for every seed or as mean and standard deviation over seeds; no seed was selected. Hyper-parameters of LightGBM and the CNN were set once from common defaults and not tuned on any target season. PLS is the primary predictor because it is the reference method of the field and is deterministic.

## 5.4 Interval methods compared

*Without reference samples* ($m=0$). (1) Gaussian band $\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\cdot$RMSECV, the implicit practice in chemometrics. (2–4) Split conformal prediction with residuals held out by sample, by lot or by season (cross-conformal with the respective folds). (5) Weighted conformal prediction for covariate shift [@tibshirani2019], with the likelihood ratio between the scans of the target lot and the source scans estimated by logistic regression on the PLS scores; it uses the unlabelled spectra of the target lot. (6–7) Conformalised quantile regression [@romano2019] with LightGBM quantile models, calibrated with sample folds or with season folds. (8) Quantile regression forest [@meinshausen2006] on PLS scores (200 trees, minimum leaf size 10). (9) Deep ensemble of five heteroscedastic CNNs trained with the Gaussian negative log-likelihood [@lakshminarayanan2017]. (10) Monte-Carlo dropout with 30 passes on one such network [@gal2016]. (11–12) LotCP and LotCP-S with $m=0$.

*With $m$ reference samples.* (a) Bias correction + source CP: the mean reference residual is subtracted and the split-conformal half-width from sample-fold residuals is kept, which combines current practice with a naive use of conformal prediction. (a′) Slope/bias correction + source CP: as (a), with the reference values of the $m$ samples regressed on their mean predictions and both slope and intercept applied ($m\ge5$). (b) Pooled recalibration: the reference residuals are added to the source calibration set. (c) Adaptive conformal inference [@gibbs2021] run over the reference samples (step size 0.05) starting from the source quantile. (d) Target-only CP: split conformal on the reference residuals alone; it is valid within the lot but requires $m\ge 1/\alpha-1$. (e) Target-only CP, centred: as (d) with scores centred on the leave-one-sample-out mean of the other reference residuals, which removes the lot bias. (f) LotCP, which for $m\ge1$ is the mean-centred hierarchical score of Lee et al. [@lee2023] applied to season-held-out residuals. (g) LotCP-S. The shrinkage versions of (f) and (g) are reported as ablations.

In addition, "spiking", that is, refitting the PLS model with the reference samples added to the training set once or replicated twenty times, is evaluated at $m=10$ with three reference draws per lot.

Methods (5)–(10) are evaluated with their own point predictions. All other methods are wrapped around the same point predictor, so differences between them are differences in calibration only.

## 5.5 Evaluation

The unit of analysis is the target lot. For every lot and every $m\in\{0,3,5,10,20,30\}$ we draw {{V:n_draws}} random reference sets and evaluate on the scans of the remaining samples; a lot is evaluated at a given $m$ only if it has at least $m+10$ samples. Within a lot every sample has equal weight and every scan equal weight within its sample, matching Eq. (6). We report: *coverage*, the weighted share of scans whose reference value lies in the interval, averaged over draws and then over lots (lot-macro coverage); the share of lot-draws with coverage below $1-\alpha-0.10$ (*under-covered lots*); the mean interval *width*; and the Winkler *interval score* [@winkler1972; @gneiting2007], $(u-l)+\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(l-y)_+ +\tfrac{2}{\alpha}(y-u)_+$, a proper score that rewards narrow intervals and penalises misses. The nominal level is $1-\alpha=0.90$; 0.80 and 0.95 are examined in Supplementary Note S1. LotCP uses $E={{V:n_episodes}}$ episodes per lot.

## 5.6 Statistical analysis

Methods are compared on the same lots, so tests are paired. For each $m$ and each competing method we apply the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [@wilcoxon1945] to the per-lot differences in interval score and in absolute coverage error $|\text{coverage}-0.90|$. The test assumes that the paired differences of lots are independent and symmetric under the null; independence across lots of the same season is an approximation, and we therefore also report in how many of the five target seasons the mean difference favours the reference method. P-values are adjusted by Holm's procedure [@holm1979] within each outcome family. The effect size is the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation [@kerby2014]. Confidence intervals for lot-macro coverage and for mean differences are 95 % percentile intervals from {{V:n_boot}} bootstrap resamples of lots, stratified by season. Because the season is the top-level cluster and there are only five target seasons, per-season results are always shown alongside pooled ones.

The experimental protocol, including hypotheses, splits, methods and metrics, was written and committed to the repository before any model was trained. Deviations are listed in Section 5.8.

## 5.7 External validation, harvest-decision simulator and economic analysis

*Untouched confirmation data.* The NeoSpectra handheld NIR soil library (Zenodo record 13122321; 1350–2550 nm; nine handheld units; {{int(r.conf('Neo','P1','n_lots'))}} evaluable target lots) was used only for the pre-registered confirmation of Section 7.3. Following the pre-registered rule, lots are 1-degree latitude–longitude cells, because fewer than 20 survey projects had at least 20 samples. The target is the KSSL estimated organic carbon, modelled as log(1 + %). Each sample has repeated scans from several units. Five-fold cross-validation over cells defines source and target, and PLS (Savitzky–Golay first derivative after SNV, 10–30 latent variables chosen by lot-grouped cross-validation) is the predictor. Its soils overlap in part with the KSSL samples of the vis–NIR analysis below, which were measured with a laboratory instrument.

*Soil vis–NIR spectra.* To test whether the findings are specific to mango, the main comparisons are repeated on visible–near-infrared spectra of soils from the Open Soil Spectral Library, version 1.2 [@safanelli2025]. Prediction of soil properties from a spectral library, adjusted with a few locally analysed samples, is an established practice in soil spectroscopy known as spiking [@guerrero2010; @guerrero2014], so the reference-sample setting is native to that field. Two subsets with a nested structure are used. (i) LUCAS, the European Union topsoil survey [@orgiazzi2018]: the model is trained on the 2009 campaign ({{f"{r.soilp('lucas_oc','n_source'):,}"}} samples) and tested on the 2015 campaign ({{f"{r.soilp('lucas_oc','n_target'):,}"}} samples); a lot is a one-degree grid cell, which stands for a region in which a few local samples would be analysed. Targets are organic carbon and pH in water. (ii) KSSL, the library of the Kellogg Soil Survey Laboratory of the United States Department of Agriculture: {{f"{r.soilp('kssl_oc','n_target'):,}"}} samples with total carbon; a lot is a survey project, and five-fold cross-validation over projects makes held-out projects the target. Carbon is modelled as $\log(1+\%)$. Spectra (400–2500 nm, thinned to 10 nm) are converted to absorbance and pre-treated by SNV and a Savitzky–Golay first derivative (11 points); PLS with 10 to 30 latent variables, chosen by lot-grouped cross-validation in the source, is the predictor. Each soil sample has one spectrum, so the replicate-scan term of Eq. (4) is not available and LotCP-S uses the reference-sample variance only. Each source set contains a single campaign or a set of projects, so there is no season level, and calibration residuals are held out by lot. Thirty reference draws per lot are used.

*Harvest-decision simulator.* For each target season, each lot and each $m\in\{0,3,5,10,20\}$, twenty random reference sets are drawn, and every non-reference fruit is classified by six rules against specifications of 14, 15 and 16 % DMC, which bracket the mean DMC of the data ({{f"{float(r.t['table1_dataset'].query('season == \"All\"').dm_mean.iloc[0]):.1f}"}} %): (i) the point prediction, $f(x)\ge t$; (ii) the bias-corrected point prediction, $f(x)+\bar r_A\ge t$; (iii) a split-conformal lower bound from sample-fold residuals ($m=0$); (iv) bias correction plus the same source margin, $f(x)+\bar r_A-q\ge t$; (v) the one-sided LotCP-S bound of Eq. (9); (vi) the one-sided LotCP-A bound. Rules (iii)–(v) are evaluated at $\alpha\in\{0.02,0.05,0.10,0.20,1/3,0.5\}$ so that complete trade-off curves can be compared, and LotCP-A at $\alpha=\delta=0.10$. Outcomes are the share of mature fruit accepted, the share of immature fruit among those accepted, the share of lots in which more than 10 % of the accepted fruit is immature, and, at lot level, the shares of not-ready lots harvested and of ready lots delayed. PLS is the primary predictor; the CNN (seed 0) is reported in the supplement.

*Economic analysis under assumed costs.* No cost data accompany the public data set, and we do not invent monetary values. Costs are expressed in units of the value $V$ of one marketable fruit: accepting an immature fruit costs $c_I=1$; withholding a mature fruit costs $c_D\in\{1/19,1/9,1/4,1/2\}$, which corresponds to $\alpha^\ast\in\{0.05,0.10,0.20,1/3\}$; each destructive reference test costs $c_R=1.5$ (the fruit plus laboratory effort of half its value); and the reference tests are spread over lots of $N\in\{200,1000,5000\}$ fruit. The expected cost per fruit of a rule is $c_I\cdot$(share of fruit immature and accepted) $+\,c_D\cdot$(share mature and withheld) $+\,m\,c_R/N$. Every number in the economic analysis is conditional on these assumptions; they are scenarios for comparing rules, not an estimate of losses in a real orchard. To check whether the cost-derived level is sensible, we also report the regret of using $\alpha^\ast$ relative to the best level on the grid chosen in hindsight.

*Decision-support prototype.* The workflow is implemented as a command-line tool (Section 10) and run on one lot of the 2021 season, chosen as the lot of median size before its result was seen.

## 5.8 Pre-specification and deviations

The experimental protocol, including hypotheses, splits, methods and metrics, was committed to the code repository before any model was trained. The main deviations are: the lot-specific scale of LotCP-S was added after inspecting one predictor on one target season (2018, which remained its worst season); the empirical-Bayes shrinkage of the bias estimate was removed after it failed on the external soil data; and the external validation, the lot-level variant LotCP-A, the harvest-decision simulator, the economic analysis, the planner and the prototype were added after the main interval results were known. Every deviation, with its timing and reason, is listed in Supplementary Note S4.
